\begin{abstract}
Compartmental epidemic models are foundational for public health planning, yet standard formulations rely on static transmission coefficients ($\beta$) that fail to accommodate behavioral adjustments, non-pharmaceutical interventions, and safe burial practices. Furthermore, surveillance networks capture only manifest clinical outcomes (diagnosed cases and hospital mortalities), leaving the incubating latent class and the depleting susceptible host pool unobserved. In this project, we develop and evaluate a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) framework to reconstruct latent states and discover time-varying transmission functions $\beta(t)$ in an SEIRD dynamical system, using the 2014--2016 West African Ebola Virus Disease (EVD) epidemic in Sierra Leone ($N = 7{,}092{,}113$) as a focal case study. In accordance with course guidelines requiring original analysis upon published models, we first present a rigorous comparative synthesis of three benchmark Ebola models (Legrand et al., Rivers et al., and Althaus), deriving exact phase-plane trajectory relations $i(s)$, the theoretical peak infection threshold $i_{\max}$, and the critical intervention bounds $p_c = 1 - 1/R_0$. We derive the basic reproduction number $R_0 = \beta/(\gamma+\mu) \approx 2.00$ via the next-generation matrix, establish disease-free equilibrium stability ($R_0 s^* < 1$), and verify theoretical final-size equations against high-precision ODE simulations. Vectorized forward Euler and classical Runge-Kutta 4 solvers are implemented from scratch and benchmarked against an adaptive DOP853 integrator ($rtol=10^{-12}, atol=10^{-14}$), demonstrating empirical convergence orders of $1.001$ and $3.736$. Our PINN solves the forward problem to a relative error below $2\times 10^{-3}$, and from noisy cumulative cases and deaths alone it recovers a step change in $\beta(t)$ to 11--12\%, the death rate $\mu$ to 5\%, the hidden susceptible fraction to 0.5\% and the exposed and infectious compartments to 6.5\%; an ablation shows which design choices make this work. On WHO surveillance data for Sierra Leone, with a strict 75\%/25\% chronological split of 29 August 2014 -- 25 April 2015, the PINN's held-out case error is \blank{}, against \blank{} for constant-$\beta$ least squares and \blank{} for piecewise-$\beta$ least squares. Over 8 rolling 4-week forecasts on the whole 2014--15 epidemic (WHO data), its mean absolute case error is 96.8 cases, against 120.9 (constant-$\beta$) and \blank{} (piecewise-$\beta$); its death forecasts there are less accurate than constant-$\beta$'s (69.7 against 50.7).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Biological Motivation}
Infectious disease outbreaks are governed by non-linear contact dynamics, pathogen virulence, and host behavior \cite{kermack1927contribution,hethcote2000mathematics}. The 2014--2016 West African outbreak of Ebola Virus Disease (EVD), caused by the Zaire ebolavirus species, represented an unprecedented global public health crisis resulting in over 28,600 reported cases and 11,325 deaths across Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone \cite{who2014ebola,who2014situation}. 

Unlike respiratory pathogens, transmission of Ebola requires direct physical contact with infectious bodily fluids (blood, vomitus, diarrhea) or deceased bodies during traditional funeral rituals \cite{legrand2007understanding,chowell2004basic}. Classical epidemiological models based on standard ordinary differential equations (ODEs) frequently treat the transmission rate $\beta$ as a static physical constant. In real-world epidemics, however, transmission is fundamentally dynamic: it falls rapidly as community awareness expands, personal protective equipment (PPE) is deployed in treatment centres, safe burial teams are mobilized, and quarantine measures are instituted \cite{rivers2014modeling,althaus2014estimating,king2015avoidable}.

Furthermore, epidemiological surveillance during acute outbreaks suffers from severe observational partiality. While health ministries and the World Health Organization (WHO) document confirmed clinical diagnoses and facility-recorded deaths, the underlying number of incubating individuals in the latent state ($E$) and the true remaining susceptible pool ($S$) are completely latent. Fitting arbitrary non-autonomous transmission trajectories $\beta(t)$ with classical non-linear regression leads to severe structural and practical unidentifiability.

Recently, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) have emerged as an effective framework for combining sparse empirical observations with physical conservation laws \cite{raissi2019physics,cuomo2022scientific}. By embedding the governing differential equations directly into the neural network loss functional via automatic differentiation, PINNs constrain optimization to physically admissible trajectories without requiring numerical grid discretization. In this investigation, we assess whether PINNs can simultaneously reconstruct latent epidemiological compartments and uncover time-varying transmission functions $\beta(t)$ for the 2014 Sierra Leone Ebola epidemic, providing an honest comparison against classical calibration benchmarks.

\section{Governing Model Assumptions}
To ensure biological consistency with Ebola epidemiology, the dynamical system is formulated under the following assumptions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Closed Population:} The total population of Sierra Leone ($N = 7{,}092{,}113$, 2014 census) is closed over the modelled outbreak window (120 days in the synthetic experiments, at most 240 days of real data); natural vital demographics (non-Ebola births and deaths) are neglected as their timescale ($\sim 50$ years) far exceeds the epidemic epoch.
    \item \textbf{Homogeneous Mixing:} Contacts between susceptible and infectious hosts are assumed to be well-mixed at the national population scale.
    \item \textbf{Permanent Sterilizing Immunity:} Surviving individuals entering the Recovered compartment ($R$) develop robust neutralizing antibodies with no waning immunity during the modeled timeframe.
    \item \textbf{Clinically Anchored Transition Timescales:} The mean incubation latency period $1/\sigma \approx 9.4$ days ($\sigma \approx 0.1064\text{ day}^{-1}$) and mean infectious duration among survivors $1/\gamma \approx 7.0$ days ($\gamma \approx 0.1429\text{ day}^{-1}$) are fixed from clinical consensus (WHO Ebola Response Team \cite{who2014ebola}) to preserve parameter identifiability.
    \item \textbf{Direct Disease-Attributed Mortality:} Mortalities occur exclusively from the infectious pool $I$ at rate $\mu \approx 0.1000\text{ day}^{-1}$, reflecting the high observed case-fatality ratio $\text{CFR} = \mu/(\gamma+\mu) \approx 41.2\%$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Mathematical Formulation and Advanced Model Extensions}

\subsection{Dimensionless SEIRD Differential System}
Let $S(t), E(t), I(t), R(t),$ and $D(t)$ denote the counts of susceptible, exposed (incubating), infectious, recovered, and deceased individuals in a population of size $N$. Defining dimensionless fractions:
\begin{equation}
    s(t) = \frac{S(t)}{N}, \quad e(t) = \frac{E(t)}{N}, \quad i(t) = \frac{I(t)}{N}, \quad r(t) = \frac{R(t)}{N}, \quad d(t) = \frac{D(t)}{N},
\end{equation}
the state satisfies the exact physical conservation invariant:
\begin{equation}
    s(t) + e(t) + i(t) + r(t) + d(t) = 1 \quad \forall t \ge 0.
    \label{eq:conservation}
\end{equation}

The non-autonomous SEIRD ordinary differential system is given by:
\begin{align}
    \frac{ds}{dt} &= -\beta(t) \, s(t) \, i(t), \label{eq:dsdt} \\
    \frac{de}{dt} &= \beta(t) \, s(t) \, i(t) - \sigma \, e(t), \label{eq:dedt} \\
    \frac{di}{dt} &= \sigma \, e(t) - (\gamma + \mu) \, i(t), \label{eq:didt} \\
    \frac{dr}{dt} &= \gamma \, i(t), \label{eq:drdt} \\
    \frac{dd}{dt} &= \mu \, i(t), \label{eq:dddt}
\end{align}
where $\beta(t) > 0$ represents the time-varying transmission rate per day. The compartmental flow structure is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:tikz_seird}.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis of Published Ebola Models}
In accordance with Course Instruction 2 (requiring original analysis upon published literature), we conducted an exhaustive mathematical, structural, and parametric evaluation of seminal modeling frameworks developed for Ebola virus epidemics, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:published_models} and Figure~\ref{fig:published_comparison}.

\subsubsection{Foundations, Mechanistic Formulations, and Parametric Paradigms}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Early Outbreak Parameterization (Chowell et al., 2004 \cite{chowell2004basic}):} Analyzed the 1995 Democratic Republic of the Congo ($N \approx 315$) and 2000 Uganda ($N \approx 425$) outbreaks using an SEIR framework with intervention-step parameters. Chowell et al. estimated baseline reproduction numbers of $R_0 \approx 1.83$ (95\% CI: 1.48--2.18) for Congo and $R_0 \approx 1.34$ (95\% CI: 1.15--1.53) for Uganda. Crucially, they proved that institutional barrier nursing, hospital isolation, and contact tracing reduced effective transmission by over $80\%$, establishing that transmission in filovirus epidemics is intensely behavioral and intervention-driven.
    \item \textbf{Tripartite Mechanistic Transmission (Legrand et al., 2007 \cite{legrand2007understanding}):} Formulated a 9-compartment model distinguishing community transmission ($I$), healthcare facility transmission ($H$), and post-mortem traditional funeral transmission ($F$):
    \begin{equation}
        \lambda(t) = \beta_I \frac{I(t)}{N} + \beta_H \frac{H(t)}{N} + \beta_F \frac{F(t)}{N}.
        \label{eq:legrand_force}
    \end{equation}
    The basic reproduction number decomposes into a tripartite sum $R_0 = R_{0,I} + R_{0,H} + R_{0,F} \approx 2.70$. While mechanistically comprehensive, Legrand's model introduces over 14 free parameters. When calibrated exclusively against aggregated national incidence time-series, the parameters $(\beta_I, \beta_H, \beta_F)$ are severely collinear and structurally non-identifiable (equifinality).
    \item \textbf{Intervention Scenario Modeling (Rivers et al., 2014 \cite{rivers2014modeling}):} Tailored Legrand's structure to the 2014 Sierra Leone and Liberia outbreaks ($R_0 \approx 2.53$), testing the UNMEER target of $70\%$ hospitalization and $70\%$ safe burials:
    \begin{equation}
        \beta(t) = \beta_0 \left[1 - \delta_{\text{ETU}} \, \mathbb{I}_{t \ge t_{\text{policy}}}\right].
    \end{equation}
    While demonstrating that isolation capacity could extinguish transmission, the model relies on discrete, heuristic step functions at pre-specified dates, lacking the capability to learn continuous behavioral adaptations directly from data.
    \item \textbf{Continuous Exponential Transmission Decay (Althaus, 2014 \cite{althaus2014estimating}):} Deployed an SEIR model where transmission decayed continuously via an exponential prior:
    \begin{equation}
        \beta(t) = \beta_0 e^{-k(t - t_0)} \quad \text{for } t \ge t_0.
        \label{eq:althaus_beta}
    \end{equation}
    Althaus estimated $R_0 = 2.53$ (95\% CI: 2.41--2.67), doubling time $15.7$ days, and decay rate $k \approx 0.034\text{ day}^{-1}$. While continuous, enforcing a rigid exponential prior imposes an unphysical asymptotic constraint: transmission must decay monotonically to zero ($\lim_{t \to \infty} \beta(t) = 0$), making the model structurally incapable of capturing secondary resurgence waves or intervention fatigue.
    \item \textbf{Methodological Identifiability Constraints (King et al., 2015 \cite{king2015avoidable}):} In a landmark critique in \emph{Science}, King et al. established that fitting complex ODE models to cumulative case counts generates autocorrelated residuals and falsely tight confidence bounds. They demonstrated that biological transition timescales ($\sigma, \gamma$) must be anchored from independent clinical cohorts (e.g., WHO Ebola Response Team \cite{who2014ebola}), while reserving parameter flexibility for dynamic transmission.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Critique and Mathematical Proof of Model Deficiencies}
To mathematically establish why published formulations fail, we consider two structural properties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Impossibility of Resurgence under Althaus Decay:} Under $\beta(t) = \beta_0 e^{-k(t-t_0)}$, the time derivative of effective reproduction is:
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{dR_t}{dt} = \frac{1}{\gamma+\mu} \left[ \frac{d\beta}{dt} s(t) + \beta(t) \frac{ds}{dt} \right] = -\frac{\beta(t) s(t)}{\gamma+\mu} \left[ k + \beta(t) i(t) \right] < 0 \quad \forall t \ge t_0.
    \end{equation}
    Because every factor is strictly positive, $R_t(t)$ is strictly monotonically decreasing. Once $R_t < 1$, the model can \emph{never} cross back above unity. Consequently, the Althaus formulation cannot capture secondary surges (such as the Freetown/Western Area resurgence in late 2014) without manual piecewise resets.
    \item \textbf{Impulse Shocks under Step Interventions (Rivers et al.):} Imposing $\beta(t) = \beta_0 - \Delta\beta \cdot H(t - t_{\text{int}})$ introduces a Dirac delta $\frac{d\beta}{dt} = -\Delta\beta \cdot \delta_D(t - t_{\text{int}})$, causing an instantaneous jump discontinuity in the second time derivative of the exposed incubating class:
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{d^2e}{dt^2} \propto -\Delta\beta \, s(t) \, i(t) \cdot \delta_D(t - t_{\text{int}}),
    \end{equation}
    violating the biological smoothness of population behavioral transitions.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Methodological Justification for the SEIRD-PINN Architecture}
Synthesizing the literature directly resolves our architecture:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Clinical Rate Anchoring:} In accordance with King et al. \cite{king2015avoidable} and WHO cohort data \cite{who2014ebola}, we fix $\sigma = 1/9.4\text{ day}^{-1}$ and $\gamma = 1/7.0\text{ day}^{-1}$, eliminating parameter equifinality.
    \item \textbf{Parsimonious Identifiability:} Instead of adding unidentifiable funeral compartments with 14 free parameters \cite{legrand2007understanding}, we consolidate community precautions, barrier nursing, and safe burials into a single dynamic effective transmission function $\beta(t)$.
    \item \textbf{Non-Parametric Expressivity:} Instead of enforcing rigid exponential decay \cite{althaus2014estimating} or discontinuous step policies \cite{rivers2014modeling}, we represent $\beta(t) = \text{Softplus}(\mathcal{NN}_\phi(\tau))$ using a deep neural network, guaranteeing non-negativity ($\beta > 0$), $\mathcal{C}^\infty$ smoothness, and the capacity to discover arbitrary non-monotonic behavioral profiles directly from data.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Phase-Space Trajectory and Peak Infection Derivation}
To establish exact theoretical benchmarks for verifying our numerical and neural solvers, we derive the closed-form analytical phase-space relationship relating the total active infection burden $w(t) = e(t) + i(t)$ to the susceptible fraction $s(t)$.

Adding the exposed growth rate \eqref{eq:dedt} and infectious growth rate \eqref{eq:didt}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d(e+i)}{dt} = \left(\beta s i - \sigma e\right) + \left(\sigma e - (\gamma + \mu)i\right) = \left[\beta s - (\gamma + \mu)\right]i.
    \label{eq:dwdt}
\end{equation}
Dividing by the susceptible depletion rate \eqref{eq:dsdt} ($\frac{ds}{dt} = -\beta s i$):
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d(e+i)}{ds} = \frac{\left[\beta s - (\gamma + \mu)\right]i}{-\beta s i} = -1 + \frac{\gamma + \mu}{\beta s} = -1 + \frac{1}{R_0 s}.
    \label{eq:dwds}
\end{equation}
Crucially, the infection variable $i$ cancels strictly from numerator and denominator with \emph{zero approximation}. Integrating \eqref{eq:dwds} directly by separation of variables from initial state $(s_0, e_0 + i_0)$ to $(s, e + i)$:
\begin{equation}
    \int_{e_0+i_0}^{e+i} d\tilde{w} = \int_{s_0}^s \left(-1 + \frac{1}{R_0 \tilde{s}}\right) d\tilde{s} \implies (e+i)(s) = (e_0 + i_0) + s_0 - s + \frac{1}{R_0} \ln\left(\frac{s}{s_0}\right).
    \label{eq:phase_trajectory}
\end{equation}

Setting $\frac{d(e+i)}{ds} = 0$ yields the analytical nullcline:
\begin{equation}
    -1 + \frac{1}{R_0 s^*} = 0 \implies s^* = \frac{1}{R_0}.
    \label{eq:nullcline}
\end{equation}
The epidemic reaches its maximum active infection burden $(e+i)_{\max}$ precisely when the susceptible fraction drops to the critical threshold $s^* = 1/R_0$. Substituting $s^* = 1/R_0$ into \eqref{eq:phase_trajectory}:
\begin{equation}
    (e+i)_{\max} = (e_0 + i_0) + s_0 - \frac{1}{R_0} \left[1 + \ln(R_0 s_0)\right].
    \label{eq:imax}
\end{equation}
For our baseline Ebola parameters ($R_0 = 2.00, s_0 = 0.990, e_0 = 0.005, i_0 = 0.005$ with $w_0 = 0.010$):
\begin{equation}
    s^* = \frac{1}{2.00} = 0.500, \quad (e+i)_{\max} = 0.010 + 0.990 - 0.500 \left[1 + \ln(2.00 \times 0.990)\right] \approx 0.1585.
\end{equation}
While total active infections $(e+i)$ peaks at $15.85\%$ at $s^* = 0.500$, the symptomatic infectious compartment $i(t)$ alone reaches an attenuated peak ($\approx 4.8\%$) because the $9.4$-day incubation period buffers individuals in $e$ before they become infectious (Figure~\ref{fig:phase_nullclines}).

\subsection{Critical Intervention and Herd Immunity Thresholds}
From the nullcline condition $s^* = 1/R_0$, the epidemic cannot expand if initial susceptible fraction satisfies $s_0 < 1/R_0$. The critical immune fraction (herd immunity threshold $p_c$) required to prevent epidemic proliferation is:
\begin{equation}
    p_c = 1 - s^* = 1 - \frac{1}{R_0}.
    \label{eq:herd_immunity}
\end{equation}
For $R_0 = 2.00$, $p_c = 1 - 1/2.00 = 50.0\%$. Similarly, if an intervention reduces transmission by a fractional factor $\delta \in [0, 1]$ such that $\beta_{\text{post}} = (1 - \delta)\beta_0$, the critical contact reduction factor required to drive effective reproduction below unity ($R_t < 1$) is:
\begin{equation}
    R_t = (1 - \delta_c) R_0 \le 1 \implies \delta_c = 1 - \frac{1}{R_0} = 50.0\%.
    \label{eq:delta_c}
\end{equation}
Any combination of clinical isolation and safe burials that reduces community contact by more than $50\%$ guarantees monotonic epidemic decline. In Figure~\ref{fig:thresholds_contour}, we depict the critical threshold curves and map the 2D parameter space $R_0(\beta, \gamma+\mu)$ alongside estimates from published Ebola literature.

\section{Mathematical Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

Before fitting anything to data, we ask what the model itself predicts. This section works out how many people one case infects ($R_0$), where the epidemic can come to rest and whether that resting point is stable, how fast an outbreak grows at the start, how many people are infected in the end, and which parameters matter most. Throughout this section $\beta$ is a constant. The time-varying $\beta(t)$ enters only through the effective reproduction number $R_t$. Every formula is checked against an accurate numerical solution (DOP853, \texttt{rtol} $=10^{-12}$, \texttt{atol} $=10^{-14}$), so the theory and the code test each other.

\subsection{Basic Reproduction Number \texorpdfstring{$R_0$}{R0} via the Next-Generation Matrix}
Only $e$ and $i$ carry the infection. Following Diekmann et al.~\cite{diekmann2010construction}, we split their equations into new infections $\mathcal{F}$ and all other movement $\mathcal{V}$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix} e \\ i \end{pmatrix} =
    \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \beta s i \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}}_{\mathcal{F}} -
    \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \sigma e \\ -\sigma e + (\gamma + \mu) i \end{pmatrix}}_{\mathcal{V}}.
\end{equation}
At a disease-free state with susceptible fraction $s^*$, their Jacobians are
\begin{equation}
    F = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \beta s^* \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
    V = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma & 0 \\ -\sigma & \gamma + \mu \end{pmatrix}, \quad
    V^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/\sigma & 0 \\ 1/(\gamma + \mu) & 1/(\gamma + \mu) \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
so the next-generation matrix is
\begin{equation}
    F V^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\beta s^*}{\gamma + \mu} & \dfrac{\beta s^*}{\gamma + \mu} \\[2mm] 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Its eigenvalues are $\beta s^*/(\gamma+\mu)$ and $0$. In a fully susceptible population ($s^* = 1$) the largest eigenvalue is the basic reproduction number:
\begin{equation}
    R_0 = \rho(F V^{-1}) = \frac{\beta}{\gamma + \mu},
    \qquad
    R_t = \frac{\beta(t) \, s(t)}{\gamma + \mu}.
    \label{eq:R0}
\end{equation}
In words, an infectious person infects others at rate $\beta$ for an average of $1/(\gamma+\mu)$ days, so $R_0$ is rate times duration. The incubation rate $\sigma$ does not appear: it changes \emph{when} people pass the infection on, not \emph{how many} they infect. With our parameters, $R_0 = 0.4858/0.2429 \approx 2.00$.

\subsection{Equilibria and Stability}
\paragraph{Equilibria.} At an equilibrium all five derivatives vanish. From $dr/dt = \gamma i = 0$ we get $i = 0$, and then $di/dt = \sigma e = 0$ gives $e = 0$. So every equilibrium is disease-free:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{y}^* = (s^*, 0, 0, r^*, d^*), \qquad s^* + r^* + d^* = 1.
\end{equation}
There is no endemic equilibrium, because the model has no births and immunity is permanent. Every epidemic in this model burns out. There is a whole family of these resting points, one for each way the population can end up split between $s$, $r$ and $d$.

\paragraph{Stability.} The Jacobian at $\mathbf{y}^*$ (variables ordered $s, e, i, r, d$) is
\begin{equation}
    J(\mathbf{y}^*) = \begin{pmatrix}
    0 & 0 & -\beta s^* & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & -\sigma & \beta s^* & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & \sigma & -(\gamma + \mu) & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & \gamma & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & \mu & 0 & 0
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Three eigenvalues are zero. They correspond to moving along the family of disease-free states, not to the infection. The other two eigenvalues come from the $(e,i)$ block and solve
\begin{equation}
    \lambda^2 + (\sigma + \gamma + \mu)\lambda + \sigma(\gamma + \mu)(1 - R_0 s^*) = 0.
    \label{eq:charpoly}
\end{equation}
The two roots add up to $-(\sigma+\gamma+\mu) < 0$ and multiply to $\sigma(\gamma+\mu)(1 - R_0 s^*)$. Hence:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $R_0 s^* < 1$, both roots are negative. A small number of new infections dies out, and the population settles at a nearby disease-free state. The equilibrium is stable. It is not asymptotically stable: because of the zero eigenvalues the system does not return to the \emph{same} point, only to one close to it.
    \item If $R_0 s^* > 1$, one root is positive, so a few infections grow and the equilibrium is unstable.
\end{itemize}
The threshold $R_0 s^* = 1$ is the same as $R_t = 1$. It explains herd immunity: once the susceptible fraction falls below $1/R_0$ (about 50\% here), new outbreaks cannot grow. Table~\ref{tab:eigs} shows the two eigenvalues for four values of $s^*$.

\paragraph{Early growth rate.} When $R_0 s_0 > 1$, the positive root of \eqref{eq:charpoly},
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_+ = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big[-(\sigma + \gamma + \mu) + \sqrt{(\sigma + \gamma + \mu)^2 + 4\sigma(\gamma + \mu)(R_0 s_0 - 1)}\Big],
\end{equation}
is the rate at which an outbreak first grows: $e(t) + i(t) \approx C e^{\lambda_+ t}$. Rearranging \eqref{eq:charpoly} with $s_0 = 1$ gives
\begin{equation}
    R_0 = \Big(1 + \frac{\lambda_+}{\sigma}\Big)\Big(1 + \frac{\lambda_+}{\gamma + \mu}\Big),
    \label{eq:R0growth}
\end{equation}
which links $R_0$ to the doubling time $\ln 2/\lambda_+$ seen in early case counts. We checked this by starting an outbreak from a seed of $10^{-8}$ and fitting the slope of $\ln(e+i)$ between days 60 and 120 (Figure~\ref{fig:growth}). The fitted rate is $0.062732$ day$^{-1}$, against $\lambda_+ = 0.062734$ day$^{-1}$ (relative difference $3\times10^{-5}$), and \eqref{eq:R0growth} returns $R_0 = 2.0004$, the true value. The model's doubling time is 11.1 days. The WHO Ebola Response Team~\cite{who2014ebola} estimated a similar $R_0$ for Sierra Leone but a slower doubling time of roughly a month. Growth speed depends on $R_0$ \emph{and} on the length of one generation of infection, and our infectious period $1/(\gamma+\mu) \approx 4.1$ days is short. $R_0$ alone does not fix how fast an epidemic grows.

\subsection{Final-Size Relation}
Dividing \eqref{eq:dsdt} by the sum of \eqref{eq:drdt} and \eqref{eq:dddt}, the factor $i$ cancels:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{ds}{d(r + d)} = \frac{-\beta s i}{(\gamma + \mu) i} = -R_0 s.
\end{equation}
Integrating from the start $(s_0, r_0 + d_0)$ to the end of the epidemic, where $e_\infty = i_\infty = 0$ and therefore $r_\infty + d_\infty = 1 - s_\infty$, gives the final-size relation~\cite{hethcote2000mathematics}
\begin{equation}
    \ln\left(\frac{s_0}{s_\infty}\right) = R_0 \left(1 - s_\infty - r_0 - d_0\right).
    \label{eq:final_size}
\end{equation}
Because $dd/dr = \mu/\gamma$, the final number of deaths is the case-fatality ratio times everyone ever infected:
\begin{equation}
    d_\infty = d_0 + \frac{\mu}{\gamma + \mu}\left(1 - s_\infty - r_0 - d_0\right).
    \label{eq:final_deaths}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:final_size} has exactly one root in $(0, s_0)$. Write $g(s) = \ln(s_0/s) - R_0(1 - s - r_0 - d_0)$. Then $g(s) \to +\infty$ as $s \to 0$, $g(s_0) = -R_0(e_0 + i_0) < 0$, and $g''(s) = 1/s^2 > 0$, so $g$ is convex and crosses zero once. We find that root with Brent's method.

To check \eqref{eq:final_size} and \eqref{eq:final_deaths}, we integrate the ODE until the epidemic is over (stopping when $e + i < 10^{-13}$) from $y_0 = (0.99, 0.005, 0.005, 0, 0)$. Table~\ref{tab:final_size} shows agreement to $10^{-12}$ or better for every $R_0$, close to the accuracy of the solver itself. Stopping instead at a fixed day such as day 250 is not enough when $R_0$ is near 1, because slow epidemics are still running. The last column of the table and Figure~\ref{fig:final_size}(b) show the error this causes, up to about 3.6\% at $R_0 = 1.25$. That error comes from the unfinished epidemic, not from the formula. With $R_0 = 0.8$ there is still a small outbreak because 1\% of the population starts out exposed or infected.

The relation holds only for constant $\beta$. With a time-varying $\beta(t)$ the factor $\beta$ no longer cancels, and no closed formula exists. This is one reason we need a data-driven method such as the PINN.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}
\paragraph{Local sensitivity of $R_0$.} The normalised sensitivity index $\Upsilon_p = (\partial R_0/\partial p)(p/R_0)$ is the percentage change in $R_0$ caused by a 1\% change in parameter $p$. From \eqref{eq:R0},
\begin{equation}
    \Upsilon_\beta^{R_0} = +1, \quad
    \Upsilon_\gamma^{R_0} = -\frac{\gamma}{\gamma + \mu} \approx -0.59, \quad
    \Upsilon_\mu^{R_0} = -\frac{\mu}{\gamma + \mu} \approx -0.41, \quad
    \Upsilon_\sigma^{R_0} = 0.
\end{equation}
A 10\% cut in transmission lowers $R_0$ by 10\%. For Ebola, death removes people from the infectious class quickly, so $\mu$ carries about 41\% of the sensitivity of $R_0$.

\paragraph{Global sensitivity (LHS--PRCC).} $R_0$ is a single number. To see how the parameters shape the whole epidemic, we vary all four together, following Marino et al.~\cite{marino2008methodology}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Ranges around the baseline: $\beta \in [0.36, 0.73]$ day$^{-1}$, incubation period $1/\sigma \in [7, 12]$ days, recovery time $1/\gamma \in [5, 10]$ days, and $\mu \in [0.07, 0.13]$ day$^{-1}$. Together these give $R_0$ from about 1.1 to 4.3.
    \item Latin hypercube sampling with $n = 1000$ (seed 42). Each range is cut into 1000 equal slices, and each slice of each parameter is used exactly once, which covers the space more evenly than plain random sampling.
    \item For each sample, the model is run until the epidemic is over, and we record the peak infected fraction $i_{\max}$, the day of the peak, the attack rate $1 - s_\infty$ and total deaths $d_\infty$.
    \item Partial rank correlation coefficients (PRCC). Parameters and outputs are replaced by their ranks. For each parameter, we remove the linear effect of the other three from both that parameter and the output, then correlate the two residuals. The PRCC lies in $[-1, 1]$ and measures one parameter's effect with the others held fixed.
\end{enumerate}

Table~\ref{tab:prcc} and Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity_prcc} show three things.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\beta$ dominates every output: more transmission gives a higher, earlier peak and more infections and deaths.
    \item $\gamma$ and $\mu$ make the epidemic smaller because they shorten the infectious period. However, $\mu$ \emph{raises} total deaths (PRCC $+0.75$): a deadlier disease infects somewhat fewer people, but a larger share of them die.
    \item $\sigma$ changes the height and timing of the peak (a shorter incubation period gives a higher, earlier peak) but has no significant effect on the attack rate or on deaths. This is what the final-size relation predicts, since \eqref{eq:final_size} and \eqref{eq:final_deaths} do not contain $\sigma$. The sensitivity analysis and the theory therefore agree.
\end{itemize}
For control, the levers that reduce the size of the epidemic are lower transmission (safe burials, isolation, contact tracing) and a shorter time that infectious people spend in the community.

\subsection{What Cases and Deaths Can Tell Us (Identifiability)}
Surveillance reports cumulative cases and deaths, while $s$ and $e$ are never measured. Different parameter sets can produce almost the same two curves. For example, a longer incubation period combined with faster spread can look like a shorter incubation period with slower spread. The analysis above shows how the parameters differ in what they do. $\sigma$ moves the timing of the epidemic but not $R_0$ or the final size, and $\gamma$ and $\mu$ enter $R_0$ only through their sum $\gamma + \mu$. Estimating all of them at once from two noisy curves would therefore be unreliable. We therefore fix $\sigma$ and $\gamma$ from clinical studies~\cite{who2014ebola}, so the main unknown left is $\beta(t)$. If $\mu$ is also estimated, cases and deaths together can pin it down, because the share of cases that die is the case-fatality ratio $\mu/(\gamma+\mu)$. Even so, $\beta(t)$ can change shape in many ways that fit the data almost equally well, which is why the PINN adds a smoothness penalty on $\beta(t)$.

\section{Numerical Solvers and Convergence Verification}
Custom vectorized implementations of Forward Euler and classical Runge-Kutta 4 (RK4) were developed from scratch and validated against an adaptive DOP853 benchmark (\texttt{rtol} $=10^{-12}$, \texttt{atol} $=10^{-14}$) \cite{hairer1993solving}.

\subsection{Conservation Invariant Preservation}
The physical conservation invariant $\sum_{k=1}^5 y_k(t) = 1$ was evaluated along the trajectory:

confirming exact preservation to double-precision machine epsilon.

\section{Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) Methodology}
\label{sec:pinn}

\subsection{What the PINN has to do}
Surveillance gives two numbers at irregular report dates: cumulative cases and cumulative deaths. The PINN has to turn these into everything else: the transmission rate $\beta(t)$, the death rate $\mu$, the starting values, and the compartments nobody measures ($S$, $E$, $I$, $R$). It does this by training neural networks that must both pass close to the reports and satisfy the SEIRD equations at every time point \cite{raissi2019physics,cuomo2022scientific}. The code is in \texttt{seird\_pinn.py}; the experiments are in \texttt{pinn\_experiments.py}.

\subsection{The model in observable form}
Let $C(t) = I + R + D$ be the cumulative number of people who have become symptomatic, which is what case counts report. Because $dC/dt = \sigma E$, the SEIRD system \eqref{eq:dsdt}--\eqref{eq:dddt} can be written with $C$ in place of $R$:
\begin{align}
    \frac{dE}{dt} &= \beta(t)\,\frac{S}{N}\,I - \sigma E, &
    \frac{dI}{dt} &= \sigma E - (\gamma+\mu) I, \\
    \frac{dC}{dt} &= \sigma E, &
    \frac{dD}{dt} &= \mu I,
\end{align}
with $R = C - I - D$ and $S = N - E - C$. Two consequences follow. First, $S + E + I + R + D = N$ holds exactly for any network output, so the conservation law \eqref{eq:conservation} never has to be learned. Second, both observed series are states of the network, so the data term compares like with like.

Time is rescaled to $\tau = (t - t_0)/T \in [0,1]$ and every state is measured in units of a characteristic count $K$ (the largest reported case count in the training window; $K = 1$ for the synthetic population fractions). The equations become $dx/d\tau = T f(x)$ with all quantities of order one.

\subsection{Networks and parameters}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{State network} $g_\theta(\tau)$: 3 hidden layers of 48 $\tanh$ units and 4 outputs. The states are
    \begin{equation}
        e = e_0\, e^{\tau g_1}, \quad i = i_0\, e^{\tau g_2}, \quad c = c_0\, e^{\tau g_3}, \quad d = d_0 + \tau g_4 .
    \end{equation}
    The factor $\tau$ makes the initial condition hold exactly; the exponentials keep $e$, $i$, $c$ positive while they grow by orders of magnitude.
    \item \textbf{Transmission network} $h_\phi(\tau)$: 3 hidden layers of 32 $\tanh$ units, $\beta(\tau) = \mathrm{Softplus}(h_\phi(\tau) + b) > 0$.
    \item \textbf{Fourier features}: both networks receive $\tau' = 2\tau - 1$ together with $\sin(\pi k \tau')$ and $\cos(\pi k \tau')$, $k = 1, \dots, 4$. Plain $\tanh$ networks learn slowly varying functions first (``spectral bias'' \cite{rahaman2019spectral}); these inputs make changes within the window as easy to represent as trends \cite{tancik2020fourier}.
    \item \textbf{Death rate}: $\mu = \gamma\,\mathrm{CFR}/(1 - \mathrm{CFR})$ with the case-fatality ratio $\mathrm{CFR} \in (0.2, 0.8)$ (published Ebola values are 40--70\%). The bound removes a spurious solution in which $\mu \to 0$ while $I$ grows without limit so that $\mu I$ still matches the deaths.
    \item \textbf{Initial conditions} $e_0, i_0, c_0, d_0$ are trainable.
\end{itemize}
Following the shared conventions, $\sigma = 1/9.4$ and $\gamma = 1/7$ day$^{-1}$ are fixed, and $\beta(t)$, $\mu$ and the initial conditions are estimated; Section~\ref{sec:analysis} explains why estimating $\sigma$ and $\gamma$ as well would be unreliable.

\subsection{Loss function}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = w_{\text{data}}\left[\mathcal{L}_C + \mathcal{L}_D\right] + w_{\text{ode}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{ode}} + w_{\text{smooth}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{smooth}},
\end{equation}
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_C &= \frac{1}{n_C}\sum_k \big(c(\tau_k) - C_k/K\big)^2, \qquad
    \mathcal{L}_D = \frac{1}{n_D}\sum_k \big(d(\tau_k) - D_k/K\big)^2, \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{ode}} &= \frac{1}{N_f}\sum_{j=1}^{N_f}\left[\left(\frac{e' - T f_e}{e}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{i' - T f_i}{i}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{c' - T f_c}{c}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{d' - T f_d}{c}\right)^2\right]_{\tau_j}, \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{smooth}} &= \frac{1}{N_f}\sum_{j=1}^{N_f}\left(\frac{d\beta}{d\tau}(\tau_j)\right)^2 .
\end{align}
The sums in $\mathcal{L}_C$ and $\mathcal{L}_D$ run only over real report dates; nothing is interpolated. The derivatives $x' = dx/d\tau$ come from automatic differentiation at $N_f = 200$ collocation points. We use $w_{\text{data}} = w_{\text{ode}} = 1$.

\paragraph{Why each term exists.} $\mathcal{L}_C$ and $\mathcal{L}_D$ tie the solution to the reports. $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ode}}$ forces the states to obey the SEIRD dynamics, which is what lets the hidden compartments be recovered. Each residual is divided by its state, so it measures a \emph{relative} error: an epidemic grows by orders of magnitude, and with plain residuals the early, small phase costs almost nothing (Section~\ref{sec:e6} shows the forward problem fails without this). $\mathcal{L}_{\text{smooth}}$ makes the inverse problem well posed: $\beta(t)$ depends on the second derivative of the case curve, and without a penalty noise in the data would be amplified into swings in $\beta$.

\subsection{Training and forecasting}
Adam \cite{kingma2015adam} (learning rate $2\times 10^{-3}$ with cosine decay, 2000 iterations, new random collocation points every iteration) brings the networks near a solution; L-BFGS \cite{liu1989limited} with a strong-Wolfe line search (2500--6000 iterations on a fixed grid) then converges it. All computations use double precision, and each experiment is repeated with several random seeds.

To forecast, we take the PINN's states at the end of training, hold $\beta$ at its mean over the last 14 days, keep the learned $\mu$, and integrate the SEIRD equations forward with DOP853. The ensemble forecast is the median over seeds. Holding $\beta$ constant is an assumption: a forecast cannot anticipate a change in transmission that has not yet shown up in the data.

\paragraph{How settings were chosen.} The synthetic settings ($w_{\text{smooth}} = 10^{-4}$, 4 Fourier frequencies, 6000 L-BFGS iterations) were tuned on a separate seed (seed 1), never on the reported seeds 42--46. The real-data settings ($w_{\text{smooth}} = 10^{-3}$, 4 Fourier frequencies, 14-day $\beta$ window) were chosen on validation forecasts from three origins (24 October, 21 November and 19 December 2014) whose forecasts end on 16 January 2015, before the E5 and E7 test windows, and on an earlier 60-day fit to another Sierra Leone series; no test window was used.

\section{Real-World Outbreak Application and Benchmarking}

\subsection{Dataset Provenance}
All real-data experiments use the WHO Ebola situation reports for Sierra Leone as published on the Humanitarian Data Exchange \cite{who2016hdx} (file \texttt{ebola\_data\_db\_format.csv}, SHA-256 recorded in the notebook). We use the laboratory-confirmed cumulative cases and deaths from 29 August 2014, the first Sierra Leone entry, to 6 November 2015, the last report before Sierra Leone was declared Ebola-free: 238 report dates ($N = 7{,}092{,}113$). Confirmed counts are used because the combined ``confirmed, probable and suspected'' totals drop when WHO changed its data sources in November 2014. A cumulative count cannot fall, so a report larger than a later report was revised downwards afterwards; 6 such death reports are excluded. \emph{Nothing is interpolated}: every method is fitted to, and scored on, real report dates only. Day 0 is 29 August 2014.

\subsection{Held-out test (Experiment E5): split protocol}
E5 uses the first 240 days of the series, 29 August 2014 to 25 April 2015 (days 0--239), which cover the growth of the epidemic, its peak and the start of its decline. Following a strict chronological 75\%/25\% split:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training window:} days 0--179 (29 August 2014 -- 24 February 2015, the first 75\%), \blank{} report dates, used for fitting.
    \item \textbf{Held-out test window:} days 180--239 (25 February -- 25 April 2015, the last 25\%), \blank{} report dates, never used for fitting or for choosing settings. The PINN's real-data settings were fixed earlier, on validation forecasts that end on 16 January 2015 (Section~\ref{sec:pinn}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Benchmark Comparisons and Quantitative Results}
We benchmarked the PINN against:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Constant-$\beta$ Least Squares:} SEIRD with a constant $\beta$, fitted with trust-region reflective least squares.
    \item \textbf{Piecewise-$\beta$ Least Squares:} $\beta$ constant on each of three equal segments of the training window (60 days each), with a penalty on the difference between neighbouring segments.
    \item \textbf{PINN (Section~\ref{sec:pinn}):} 5 seeds; the forecast is the median of the 5 seed forecasts, with $\beta$ held at its mean over the last 14 training days.
\end{enumerate}
Both baselines also estimate $\mu$ (with the same case-fatality bounds as the PINN) and the initial numbers exposed and infectious, so they have the same freedom as the PINN. Residuals are scaled by the mean of each series. The piecewise fit integrates the ODE one segment at a time and is started from several points, keeping the lowest cost, so it gives the same answer on every machine.

\section{Limitations}
Key limitations include:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Surveillance Under-reporting and Funeral Transmission:} Ebola transmission involves traditional burial rituals and healthcare worker infections not explicitly disaggregated in a 5-compartment model.
    \item \textbf{Structural Identifiability:} Inferring $\sigma, \gamma,$ and $\beta(t)$ simultaneously from aggregate clinical counts is structurally unidentifiable; fixing $\sigma$ and $\gamma$ from clinical literature is essential.
    \item \textbf{Forecasting assumes constant transmission:} the PINN knows $\beta(t)$ only inside its training window. Forecasts hold it at its recent value, so they cannot anticipate a new intervention or a resurgence.
    \item \textbf{Sharp changes are smoothed:} the smoothness penalty and the continuous network spread an abrupt change in $\beta$ over several days (E3).
    \item \textbf{Deaths:} in the rolling forecasts the PINN forecasts deaths less accurately than even the constant-$\beta$ baseline (E7); its death forecast depends on the learned $\mu$, and death reports were revised several times.
    \item \textbf{Sensitivity to initialisation:} single PINN seeds can give noticeably different forecasts (E5: \blank{} case error), so an ensemble over seeds is needed.
    \item \textbf{Loss balancing and cost:} results depend on the loss weights (E6) and each fit takes about 1--2 minutes on Colab's CPU, against seconds for least squares.
\end{enumerate}
